’Tis the season. In mathematics, it’s the season of declarations by various groups of mathematicians; we’ve seen the [Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics](https://leidendeclaration.ai/), the declaration of 25 Fields medalists on “[A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics](https://mathandai.org/),” and more. Individual mathematicians are posting their own AI statements for graduate students.

I have not endorsed any of these statements and declarations, nor am I planning to for the reasons discussed by Tim Gowers [here](https://gowers.wordpress.com/2026/07/26/thoughts-about-the-leiden-declaration/) and [here](https://gowers.wordpress.com/2026/09/17/why-i-didnt-sign-the-fields-medallists-letter/), but in the spirit of the season, I would like to make my own AI declaration. I do it because in academia, we love judging other people. Throughout the year, I serve on editorial boards of some journals, referee papers for others, nominate or select people for awards, evaluate conference proposals, and select participants for the conferences I am organizing. In the fall of each year, I write tens of reference letters for grad school, postdoc, and job applications, tenure cases, and other things. I’m also on the phone with hedge funds that cannot merely rely on a letter and instead need a 30-minute conversation about the applicant before making their final decision. Come winter and spring, I need to read grant proposals and grad school, postdoc, and tenure-track applications. Not to mention that throughout this whole time I teach and must evaluate students almost all the time by grading their quizzes, assignments, midterms, and finals. Given that, those on the other side deserve to know the principles upon which my evaluation is based. My comments are limited to research, leaving teaching and supervision aside.

To state my principles clearly, I looked for guidance to the documents about the research mission of academia. A natural place to start was my own university’s [Statement of Principles](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/principles/statement-of-principles/), joint with Washington University in St. Louis. It opens with: “The core purpose of a research university is the creation, preservation and dissemination of knowledge.” OECD’s [Frascati Manual](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/frascati-manual-2015_9789264239012-en.html) defines research and development as “creative and systematic work undertaken in order to increase the stock of knowledge – including knowledge of humankind, culture and society – and to devise new applications of available knowledge.” Finally, the [US National Science Foundation](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/merit-review) evaluates proposals based on two criteria: intellectual merit and broader impacts. The intellectual merit is defined as “[t]he potential for the proposed project to advance knowledge and understanding within its own field or across different fields,” while the broader impacts refer to “[t]he potential for the proposed project to benefit society and contribute to the achievement of specific, desired societal outcomes.” These statements identify the goal of research, but stay silent on the method. If two papers add equally to knowledge and understanding and meet the same standards of correctness and research ethics, I see no reason to value one less because its author used AI.

With that, I take my job as a researcher as fulfillment of the following mission statement:

> The mission of academic research is the production of knowledge. The value of a research contribution lies primarily in the new knowledge and understanding it produces, rather than in the methods or agents responsible for its production.

Consequently, when evaluating a paper, my evaluation will be independent of whether it was written with or without the help of AI. There should be no reward for not using AI in research; any tool is fine as long as you advance knowledge and understanding. I stress that this is constrained to research; in contrast, when learning mathematics, students need to be able to do certain tasks themselves without using AI. Of course, the permission to use any methods is qualified by ethical considerations – I wouldn’t engage in cruel treatment of animals or accept donations from Jeffrey Epstein-like characters to pursue research (or any other purpose, for that matter). However, objections to a particular use of AI should identify the ethical principle at issue, just as they would for any other research method.

Before moving on, I want to address one criticism, as production of knowledge is often conflated with production of papers. Arguably, we don’t need more papers; ask the arXiv moderators, for example. Papers are, however, an efficient way to communicate complex ideas. To my surprise, many public discussions about the future of mathematics recommend emphasizing interviews and/or talks over their written work. An interview can help establish whether an applicant understands their work, but the value of that work shouldn’t depend on how impressive its author is in person. At some point, you’ll die, taking your collegiality and interpersonal skills with you. Your papers will live on, allowing people to engage with your ideas for decades or centuries afterwards. I’ll summarize this digression by reiterating that the goal of research is the production of knowledge and understanding; the process of building understanding is best served by communicating one’s findings through written records; and neither of these statements endorses maximizing some proxy metrics (number of papers/citations, journal prestige, etc.).

Perhaps we are overdue for a rethink of what counts as a research contribution. To bring up a specific example I was involved in recently, a student had worked on a problem for a year, deliberately avoiding the use of AI, but hadn’t solved it. Their friend then gave the problem to Sol 6.1, which produced a solution within an hour; the student checked it and found it correct. The prompt wasn’t elaborate – it simply stated the problem given to the student by their advisor. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the student had solved the problem independently after a year of work. What would have been the value of their contribution? To be clear, this would have been a paper-worthy contribution in the pre-AI era, but now, all that the student would have done was advance knowledge as much as a one-hour ChatGPT session, which they were simply unwilling to use. I see the benefits of this process to the student, like deepening their understanding of the problem and improving their problem-solving skills. In NSF’s terms, that supports a case for broader impacts. It does not, by itself, establish the intellectual merit of the research. Looking ahead, what justifies funding a new 12-month research project if the same research contribution can be obtained in a day?

I want to acknowledge that this situation stings, and no-one feels this pain more than graduate students. This is why right now we must recognize the important pre-AI contributions of those without job security. Every person that spent years or months working on a project only to enter a job market where AI can produce a solution in hours or minutes can count on my full support. But this cannot continue indefinitely; I would consider it irresponsible to give new graduate students projects that can be solved by AI in a day.

The distinction between personal motivations for doing math and societal motivations for funding it is perhaps at the heart of the current debate on the future of mathematics. Many of us went into math for aesthetic reasons; we view it almost as art. But “I find it beautiful” is a rather thin justification for asking someone else to pay my salary. From the societal perspective, a paper of mine may have only a handful of readers and still make a contribution worth supporting. The case for that support rests on what the work adds to knowledge and understanding.

Society funds math because it is useful. This is not to say that pure math should not exist; quite the opposite. In “The Usefulness of Useless Knowledge,” Abraham Flexner gives examples of how research pursued without practical goals led to unforeseen scientific and technological applications. Mathematics offers examples of its own, including elliptic curves in cryptography and non-Euclidean geometry in GPS. That said, the contributions made by mathematicians must advance knowledge and understanding. Spending a year on a problem does not make the resulting contribution more valuable if an available tool can produce the same knowledge and understanding in an hour.

I see no reason for a blanket AI declaration in every mathematical paper. A disclosure should give readers information they need to assess the work, just as it should for other computational tools. Some journals require AI statements, and when asked, I provide them in as much detail as possible. As an editor or a referee, I treat a contribution obtained using AI just as I would one obtained without it. Some prominent mathematicians have been publicly critical of AI, and their statements leave me uncertain whether they would do the same. If authors expect to be penalized simply for using AI, they have an incentive to conceal that use. Making honesty a professional liability is a poor way to build trust in a research community. Journals should make clear whether AI use, by itself, will count against a submission.

What I hope I have established is that, with the arrival of new technologies, we have to adapt our way of doing mathematics to continue to fulfill the mission statement above. The tools available today give us reason to rethink how we do research and evaluate contributions. Large language models can already solve hard problems, and they are only getting better. They don’t write well, they don’t generate good abstractions, and they don’t ask good research questions. It’s unclear whether any of these statements will be true in a month, or a year, or five years; but it is reasonable to assume that the models will get better at all these things. Analyzing and explaining proofs produced by AI can itself advance understanding. But the future of mathematics should involve more than making sense of what the models produce.

Despite that, I would say that this is an exciting time to be a mathematician. The field must reinvent itself, and all of us can be a part of that. Things will change; for example, I’m confident that every mathematician will need to know some machine learning and some formal verification. We should be able to adapt models to specific mathematical tasks and explore alternatives to general-purpose models whose future pricing is uncertain. Working with papers formalized in Lean will require us to read the code and understand what has actually been proved. Our graduate programs will have to prepare students for that, and do so quickly. The adjustment period might be turbulent and we must protect the most vulnerable among us, but ultimately we will be able to reach new levels of understanding, new levels of abstraction, and bridge the gap between pure and applied mathematics, finding new applications of areas we haven’t invented yet.

Sadly, many mathematicians equate the necessity of change with the end of mathematics. Describing how capitalism can come to seem like the only viable economic and social system, literary theorist Fredric Jameson wrote that it is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism. I find something similar to be true about many mathematicians – they find it easier to imagine the end of mathematics than a possible change to our workflow.

For comparison, computer science had to reinvent itself multiple times during its short existence. Computer scientists constantly ask what a CS degree should prepare students to do, what the value of their work is, et cetera. They talk to each other and, more importantly, they talk to the external stakeholders (industry, government). For years now, mathematicians have perfected the process of educating copies of themselves. The time has come for a debate about the future of the field. That debate should inform what we fund, whom we hire, and what we train students to do. It needs to involve people without PhDs and those without fancy positions at top universities. Arguably, the debate among non-mathematicians will inevitably happen; as mathematicians, we will like its outcomes way better if we have a seat at their table or, better yet, invite them to ours.
